Clinical Education Hub

Marketing asked for AI that summarizes clinical papers for sales reps. After talking to the reps, I built something different: a searchable library that matches how reps actually learn.

The requestWhat I was asked for

Neurovascular marketing wanted AI-generated summaries of clinical studies so reps could get through the evidence faster.

The real problemWhat research showed

I interviewed 25+ reps, ran field surveys, audited the ShowPad content library, and sat in on sales training. The clinical content was accurate. Reps just couldn't find it, couldn't save it, and couldn't learn from it in the formats it came in.

That made it a retrieval and application problem, not a summarization problem.

What I shippedScope and tradeoffs

A searchable multimedia hub, designed and built in a 4-day sprint:

  • Clinical summaries with citations back to the source
  • Audio versions for reps on the road
  • Illustrated snapshots and flashcards for different learning styles

I deliberately left AI features out of the MVP until the core experience was validated with reps.

The outcomeWhat happened next

Leadership selected the Hub for company-wide rollout to 4,000+ sales and marketing employees, and turned my internship project into a contract product-owner role.

Where it's goingCurrent roadmap

I own requirements and the roadmap, aligning marketing, compliance, and IT on content ownership, review, and platform integration. The phased AI plan adds semantic search and a citation-grounded evidence assistant that declines to answer when the evidence is thin. Success is measured by time-to-evidence and citation accuracy.

How I work

Three habits, each learned on a real project.

Diagnose before building

The Hub started as a request for AI summaries. Interviews showed the problem was finding and using evidence, which changed what we built.

Ship the smallest thing that proves it

A 4-day MVP without AI earned the trust, and the company-wide rollout, to build AI features next.

AI should know when to say no

In clinical settings a confident wrong answer is worse than none. The evidence assistant declines when sources are thin, and citation accuracy is a success metric.
